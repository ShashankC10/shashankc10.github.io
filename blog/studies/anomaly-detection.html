Anomaly Detection Starts with Defining Normal (Anomaly Detection)

Shashank Chandavarkar · July 11, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026 · 12 min read · companion PDF

I went into this study expecting to compare algorithms. The question I keep returning to is what “normal” means for the particular system, and what someone should do when a detector says it has changed. The algorithm matters, but it can't settle those questions on its own.

Decide what deserves attention

Anomaly detection looks for observations or patterns that differ unexpectedly from a reference. A sequence such as 72, 74, 71, 73, 72, 74, 180 makes the final reading visually obvious. Even then, its meaning depends on the signal, measurement process, and context. A surprising number is a reason to investigate, not a diagnosis.

Applications include payment activity across several countries in a short period, CPU or latency changes, unusual manufacturing vibration, abrupt financial-market moves, and a user suddenly adding hundreds of items to a cart. Each could reflect an incident, a legitimate change, or bad telemetry. I'd define which of those outcomes the system is meant to find.

Before selecting a model, I'd ask whether the data is a time series, tabular events, or a snapshot; whether reliable incident labels exist; how quickly detection needs to happen; how costly a miss is relative to a false alarm; and how rare the target incidents actually are. If positives are scarce, accuracy can reward a detector that never alerts.

Three different ways of being unusual

A point anomaly is an individual observation that is extreme against a reference. CPU readings of 45, 47, 46, 45, 98, 46, 45 give a clear candidate to inspect. The point is statistically unusual; the workload still determines whether it's harmful.

A contextual anomaly is plausible in general but unexpected for this entity, time, or state. Ninety percent CPU might be ordinary during a weekday batch job and surprising at 3 a.m. on Sunday. It could also be a scheduled overnight backup. Time of day helps only when the reference actually represents the workload.

A collective anomaly is an unusual sequence or group whose individual values need not be extreme. CPU reported as exactly 65% for six hours could deserve attention even if 65% is ordinarily fine. I'd inspect a steady workload, stuck telemetry, rounding, or a process change rather than assume the flat line identifies one cause.

Start with a defensible statistical baseline

I'd inspect the mean, spread, shape, and stability of representative normal operation before picking thresholds. For a Gaussian distribution, roughly 68% lies within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three. About 0.3% is outside three standard deviations. That probability comes from a distributional assumption, not a universal property of healthy systems.

z = (x - μ) / σ
Three-sigma flag: |x - μ| > 3σ

If μ = 45 and σ = 10:
  three-sigma interval = [15, 75]
  z for x = 98 is (98 - 45) / 10 = 5.3

CPU is bounded and often seasonal, so this is an illustrative calculation. Skew, heavy tails, or a contaminated reference period can make a three-sigma rule generate the wrong queue. Even a small per-point false-alarm probability can become a large volume across many metrics.

Robust alternatives reduce sensitivity to extreme observations:

MAD = median(|x_i - median(x)|)
Modified z = 0.6745 × (x - median(x)) / MAD

Q1 = 25th percentile
Q3 = 75th percentile
IQR = Q3 - Q1
Lower fence = Q1 - 1.5 × IQR
Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

The modified z-score uses the median and median absolute deviation. The scaling constant connects MAD with a Gaussian standard-deviation scale. I'd choose and validate its threshold rather than assume every “z” uses the same cutoff. A zero MAD or standard deviation needs explicit handling.

IQR fences are the familiar box-plot rule. They don't require Gaussian data, but that doesn't make every point beyond a fence meaningful. Skew, multiple populations, and context affect how useful the flags are.

Relationships can matter more than individual values

CPU at 70% and memory at 95% might each occur in normal operation, yet their combination could be unusual. Mahalanobis distance accounts for covariance between features:

D² = (x - μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x - μ)

x  = vector of observed features
μ  = baseline mean vector
Σ  = baseline covariance matrix

It generalizes a standardized distance to multiple dimensions. I'd scale and estimate the reference carefully; a singular covariance matrix, outliers in the fit, or several distinct normal clusters can make a simple global distance misleading. An unseen combination isn't necessarily far under this geometry, so I'd test whether it captures the relationships that matter.

Give the model the history it needs

For time series, I'd start with a few explicit context features:

  • Recent level and variability: rolling mean and standard deviation over the preceding five minutes, or another window matched to the system.
  • Change: current value minus the value five minutes earlier. Dividing by elapsed time gives a rate of change.
  • Rolling z-score: distance from the recent mean in units of recent standard deviation.
  • Seasonal comparison: current latency relative to a baseline for the same hour yesterday, with zero and missing baselines handled.
delta = x_t - x_(t-5min)
rolling_z = (x_t - preceding_window_mean)
            / preceding_window_std
latency_ratio = current_latency
                / baseline_same_hour_yesterday

For transactions or account activity, I'd compare amount with the user's earlier average, calculate time since the last transaction and transactions in the past hour, and include new-country and new-device indicators. Three transactions in ten seconds can be informative; it can also be a retry bug. I'd inspect the events behind the count.

Past-only features must exclude the current outcome and future observations. A model that scores independent rows can still use time through engineered features; it doesn't discover order that isn't represented in its input.

One reading, two baselines

Suppose a service normally runs at 30% CPU overnight and 80% during its afternoon job. A global threshold of 75% alerts every afternoon. Raising it to 95% stops those alerts but misses an unexpected overnight reading of 70%.

Against the overnight baseline, 70% has a residual of forty percentage points. Against the afternoon baseline, 85% has a residual of five. I'd still account for ordinary variability in each context, but now the comparison reflects the question I meant to ask. I'd calculate the reference from earlier data and replay it on later periods before deciding how many of those residuals should page someone.

Catch a sustained shift with CUSUM

A single threshold can miss a small change that persists. An upward CUSUM accumulates deviations above a reference allowance:

S_t = max(0, S_(t-1) + (x_t - μ - k))
Alert if S_t > h

Here μ is the reference mean, k is an allowance related to the shift of interest, and h is the decision threshold. A downward counterpart can detect sustained decreases. The intuition is a metric that stays a little high for many readings eventually accumulating enough evidence. I'd tune it against the desired detection delay and false-alarm rate, not merely pick a large-looking cumulative sum.

How the unsupervised models define unusual

Isolation Forest repeatedly chooses random features and split points. Observations isolated after relatively few splits have shorter average path lengths and can receive higher anomaly scores. The original score uses a normalized exponential transform, not simply one divided by path length:

s(x, n) = 2^(-E[h(x)] / c(n))

E[h(x)] = average isolation path length
c(n)    = normalization for sample size n

It's a useful tabular baseline and can be computationally efficient. Many irrelevant dimensions can still make detection difficult. It scores the supplied feature vectors, so sequence effects need lag features, residuals, or another representation. Library score signs vary; the IsolationForest documentation explains its interface and path-length interpretation.

Local Outlier Factor (LOF) compares local reachability density with that of neighboring observations. A score near one suggests similar local density; a substantially larger score suggests the point is sparse relative to its neighbors. It can distinguish local anomalies in populations with different densities. Nearest-neighbor search, feature scaling, and neighborhood size all matter, especially at scale. Scoring new unseen observations also needs the implementation's novelty-detection mode where applicable.

Autoencoders learn to compress and reconstruct representative normal data. For example, an input of [CPU 45%, memory 60%, latency 200 ms] might reconstruct as [44%, 61%, 201 ms]. A small scaled reconstruction error is expected for familiar patterns; a large error can be an anomaly signal.

latent = encoder(input)
reconstruction = decoder(latent)
score = mean((scaled_input - reconstruction)²)

Scaling matters because milliseconds and percentages shouldn't compete accidentally. The network can handle complex multivariate inputs, including sequences or images with suitable architectures, but it may also reconstruct some anomalies well. An LSTM autoencoder introduces temporal structure. I'd compare it with simpler baselines, check contamination in training, and remember that reconstruction error rarely explains the cause of an incident.

One-Class SVM learns a boundary around a reference distribution, commonly by separating the mapped observations from the origin in a kernel feature space. It can work for smaller datasets, but kernel choice, scaling, and hyperparameters such as nu matter. Kernel implementations can be expensive on large datasets; alternatives with different scalability exist.

Use labels when they are trustworthy

If incident labels exist, I'd compare supervised models such as XGBoost or LightGBM with unsupervised scores. A classifier can learn the anomalies represented by those labels, but might miss a new kind of incident. On data with 99% normal observations, an always-normal model gets 99% accuracy and detects nothing.

Class weights change the training penalty assigned to positive examples. A weight of one hundred is a modeling choice, not automatically an accurate statement of business cost. SMOTE synthesizes minority examples by interpolation; I'd check whether those combinations are meaningful for the data, use it only within training folds, and evaluate on untouched future observations.

For time series, model the expected pattern first

STL separates y_t = trend_t + seasonal_t + residual_t. I'd inspect the residual for unexplained changes rather than flag predictable daily peaks. A three-sigma residual threshold is one candidate if its assumptions fit. Residuals aren't guaranteed to be independent Gaussian noise, and a decomposition fitted using future observations would leak information into a simulated online detector.

ARIMA or Prophet can produce forecasts with prediction intervals. If the predicted CPU is 45% and a chosen interval is [35%, 55%], an observed 92% lies outside it and becomes a candidate for review. That is a prediction interval for a future observation, not a confidence interval for a mean. A nominal 99% interval needs coverage checks and still permits misses by design.

My model shortlist would follow the available evidence: a supervised classifier for reliable labels, STL residuals for a seasonal baseline, a forecasting model when intervals matter, Isolation Forest for unlabeled tabular data, an autoencoder when multivariate reconstruction is useful, a sequence model for patterns over time, or One-Class SVM for a suitable smaller reference set. I'd evaluate each against the actual incident types rather than designate one as the most accurate in advance.

Evaluate the queue as well as the score

Precision = true alerts / all alerts
Recall = detected incidents / all labeled incidents
F1 = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)

With labels, I'd use temporal validation, precision-recall curves, and an appropriate PR summary such as average precision. I'd also define whether matching is point-based or incident-based: twenty alerts during one outage aren't twenty separately detected incidents. Detection delay matters when a correct alert arrives too late.

Without labels, synthetic injections can test whether the detector catches the patterns I deliberately introduce, but don't prove real-world recall. Human review estimates how useful the alert queue is; it doesn't reveal incidents outside that queue. I'd inspect samples of unflagged periods too and retain uncertainty about the unknown ground truth.

For a score where larger means more anomalous, lowering the threshold increases the alert set and can improve recall while adding false alarms. Precision needn't change monotonically at every step. Some tools reverse score direction, so I'd check the outlier-detection interface before tuning thresholds.

The choice depends on the cost of a miss, required response time, reviewer capacity, and feedback. I'd start conservatively where appropriate and validate the queue people can actually handle, rather than choose a threshold just because it flags a tidy percentage.

Plan for online state and changing normal

Real-time detection scores arriving events within a latency budget. Batch detection can inspect windows for a daily review and may afford heavier sequence models. A z-score or Isolation Forest can be fast at scoring, but rolling means and velocity features still require state. “Fast model” doesn't mean “stateless system.”

I'd represent seasonality through a contextual baseline, separate models where there is enough data, or time and workload features. A server regularly busy from 9 to 5 shouldn't page someone solely because noon is busy. Whether a particular model uses those context features well must be checked.

Growth, launches, and infrastructure changes can shift the reference. A rolling thirty-day training window is one possible response, but could lose annual seasonality or absorb an ongoing incident. I'd monitor behavior, review what enters the training set, and choose the window against the time scales I need to preserve.

Alert fatigue needs explicit design. Fifty noisy alerts a day can destroy trust. I'd group related CPU, memory, and latency changes when evidence supports a common incident, separate urgent severity from a morning-review queue, and record resolutions. Correlation can suggest shared context; it doesn't prove a shared root cause. Those outcomes should inform thresholds, features, and retraining.

How I'd explain my first pass

I'd define the incident and its cost, establish whether labels exist, and build a context-aware statistical baseline. Then I'd compare suitable detectors, replay them on later periods, inspect both alerts and misses, and agree on the response policy. The system has to keep working as normal changes, and the people receiving its alerts need a reason to trust them.
