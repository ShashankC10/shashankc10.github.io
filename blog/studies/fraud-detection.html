Fraud Detection Has to Make a Decision (Fraud Detection)

Shashank Chandavarkar · September 29, 2026 · Updated September 30, 2026 · 10 min read · companion PDF

A fraud score has to turn into something: let the payment through, ask the customer to verify it, or decline it. That's what makes this problem feel different from an ordinary classification exercise. A false positive happens to someone who was trying to buy something.

Start with the decision and the adversary

Fraud detection covers card payments, account takeovers, returns, insurance claims, advertising bots, and fraudulent app installs. They share the problem of deceptive behavior, but not the same evidence or deadline. A payment authorization may have a tight real-time budget; an insurance investigation can use a slower batch process.

I'd clarify the fraud type, scoring latency, available labels, and the costs of missed fraud and false alarms. I'd also ask which actions are available. Verification and investigator review give the system alternatives to immediate approval or rejection.

The adversarial part changes the lifecycle. Once a pattern is blocked, attackers can change timing, amounts, merchants, or devices. A model can become less useful even if the application and data pipeline haven't changed. That makes recent outcomes and investigation feedback important.

Rare events make accuracy misleading

In an illustrative population with 200 fraudulent payments among 100,000 transactions, approving everything achieves 99.8% accuracy and catches no fraud. Rates such as 0.1% or 2% represent quite different workloads; I'd estimate the prevalence for the actual product, time period, and label definition.

I'd explore amount, local time, merchant category, and velocity. Small test charges and large attempted withdrawals are patterns worth looking for, but neither establishes a universal fraud distribution. A $1 payment could be ordinary, and a $3,000 purchase could be legitimate. Electronics, jewelry, gift cards, and transfers are categories to inspect, not labels. Nor would I assume every market's fraud peaks between 3 and 5 a.m.

The sequence can add information that the individual amounts lack. Compare a hypothetical day with coffee at 8 a.m., lunch at noon, and groceries at 6 p.m. against four attempts at 3:01, 3:03, 3:05, and 3:07, for $200, $800, $500, and $2,000. That burst deserves investigation. It still needs context: a batch of legitimate purchases or duplicated events can also look busy.

Build the history around each transaction

I'd organize the feature set into a few groups, keeping every historical value strictly available before the decision:

  • Transaction: amount, merchant category, country, hour, and an international flag relative to the account's usual geography.
  • Personal baseline: amount relative to the preceding thirty-day mean and historical maximum, plus whether the merchant is new. A $5,000 transaction means something different for an account usually spending $20 than for one with a long history of large purchases.
  • Velocity: transactions and declines in the last hour, unique countries in the last day, and shorter sliding windows where quick bursts matter.
  • Device and network: a new-device flag, network characteristics such as VPN or Tor use, and the number of accounts associated with a device.
  • Graph relationships: links among accounts, devices, and merchants, including counts of previously confirmed fraud accounts sharing a device and distance to a known fraud node.

I'd guard against zero denominators and missing history rather than invent extreme ratios. A device shared by several people might belong to a household, and VPN use has legitimate explanations. Connections should contribute evidence; they aren't proof.

“Impossible travel” is another useful but imperfect feature. Two apparent in-person transactions in New York and London, about 5,570 km apart, within forty-five minutes conflict with physical travel. But merchant geography, payment routing, time zones, remote purchases, and geolocation errors can break that interpretation. I'd establish what the location field measures before using distance divided by elapsed time.

Historical graph features need a time cutoff too. If an account was only confirmed fraudulent next week, today's feature must not act as though that verdict was already known.

Bayes keeps the base rate in the calculation

A suspicious signal isn't the same as a high posterior probability. Bayes' theorem relates the likelihood of the evidence under fraud to the prior fraud rate:

P(fraud | x) = P(x | fraud) × P(fraud) / P(x)

Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds
Likelihood ratio = P(x | fraud) / P(x | legitimate)

With a 0.2% prior, prior odds are 0.002 / 0.998. A likelihood ratio of 50 gives posterior odds of about 0.1002, or a probability of roughly 9.1%. The signal is strong relative to the prior, but the event is still far from certain. “Fifty times suspicious” only supports that calculation if it really means a likelihood ratio.

Derive the threshold from explicit costs

Suppose allowing fraud costs $500, declining a legitimate payment costs $30, and the other outcomes cost nothing. This deliberately simplified example gives a useful threshold calculation:

p = calibrated fraud probability
Expected cost of allowing = 500 × p
Expected cost of blocking = 30 × (1 - p)

Block when 500 × p > 30 × (1 - p)
Therefore p > 30 / 530 ≈ 5.7%

Those costs aren't a recommended policy. Real losses, recovery rates, customer friction, and verification costs vary. Higher-value transactions may justify a lower probability threshold if the expected loss rises while other costs stay similar. I'd derive that relationship rather than declare that every transaction over $5,000 needs the same treatment.

A score that ranks cases well may still be poorly calibrated. I'd check whether cases scored near 0.1 are fraudulent roughly 10% of the time on representative held-out data. Class weighting and sampling can affect probability estimates. The scikit-learn calibration guide explains reliability diagrams and the distinction between discrimination and calibration.

Compare rules, linear models, and trees

Rules make a useful reference system: an amount ten times the user's recent average, five attempts in an hour, an unfamiliar international merchant, or a known malicious IP could trigger review. I'd validate each rule and its action, not automatically block every match. Rules are fast and inspectable, but brittle, maintainable only up to a point, and open to adaptation.

Logistic regression combines features into log-odds and converts them to a probability:

z = bias + w₁ × amount + w₂ × new_country
         + w₃ × velocity + ...
P(fraud) = sigmoid(z) = 1 / (1 + exp(-z))

A hypothetical coefficient of +2.3 on a new-country indicator increases modeled log-odds, while -1.5 on a familiar-merchant indicator reduces them, holding the other features fixed. Scale, correlated inputs, and interactions affect interpretation. Coefficients explain the fitted relationship, not a causal mechanism or a complete decision rationale.

XGBoost and LightGBM build trees sequentially to improve the training objective. They can capture interactions such as an unusual amount combined with a new device. A positive-class weight based on n_legitimate / n_fraud is a starting experiment, not necessarily the best setting. I'd tune using the intended operating metric and check calibration afterward.

SHAP values can help investigators understand which inputs moved a particular model score relative to a reference. They don't by themselves establish legal compliance, causal explanations, or appropriate wording for a customer. I'd keep internal investigative detail separate from clear, useful customer guidance.

Handle imbalance without distorting the test

Class weights are one option. Undersampling reduces the legitimate examples used in training, trading volume for possible information loss. An ensemble can train models on different subsets and average their predictions; fifty such models is a possible experiment, not a required architecture.

SMOTE interpolates between minority examples to synthesize training observations. In fraud data, that can create implausible combinations of transaction amount, merchant, and device features. I'd compare it carefully with weighting, and apply any sampling only within training folds. Validation and test sets should preserve the deployment distribution and temporal order.

When sequences and graphs earn their complexity

An LSTM can learn from a sequence of transactions rather than only aggregated counts. A supervised sequence classifier may recognize patterns associated with fraud; a reconstruction-based detector might instead flag deviations. An unusual sequence isn't automatically malicious, so the objective and labels still matter.

A graph neural network passes information along account-device-merchant edges. With two local message-passing rounds, a node can receive information from up to two hops away. That may expose coordinated behavior missed by isolated transaction features. It also introduces graph freshness, label leakage, shared-device ambiguity, and additional serving complexity. I'd first test whether simple graph features already deliver the benefit.

Evaluate the actual operating point

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Recall    = TP / (TP + FN)
F1        = 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall)
False positive rate = FP / (FP + TN)
Miss rate = FN / (TP + FN) = 1 - recall

Precision is the share of flagged cases that are fraud. It is not the false-positive rate, which uses all legitimate cases as its denominator. Recall is the share of fraud caught. F1 balances precision and recall but doesn't incorporate transaction value or the full cost of customer friction.

A precision-recall curve and its area are useful with rare positives. I'd state whether I report average precision or trapezoidal PR area, since they aren't identical. Alongside that, I'd report fraud dollars missed, legitimate declines, verification volume, and reviewer workload at the selected thresholds.

Labels can arrive long after a payment. A recent transaction with no complaint isn't necessarily confirmed legitimate. I'd use later evaluation periods with sufficiently mature outcomes, tracking the delay and selection effects in investigator labels. Blocked transactions also create missing counterfactuals: I don't observe what would have happened had they been approved.

Budget the complete decision path

Suppose the product sets a 300 ms deadline. An illustrative budget might allow 10 ms for historical features from Redis, 20 ms for velocity features, 5 ms for rules, 15 ms for model inference, and 5 ms for the decision policy. Those components sum to 55 ms, or roughly 60 ms after small overheads. The remaining budget must cover network variation, serialization, queues, and other work. These are planning numbers, not measured guarantees.

I'd measure P95 and P99 end-to-end latency, not just an isolated cache operation. Redis can support fast serving, but networked requests and failure paths matter. Duplicate events, retries, and delayed streams can corrupt window counts; missing features need an agreed fallback.

A three-way policy could approve below threshold A, request step-up verification between A and B, and decline above B. An illustrative score partition is below 0.3, from 0.3 to below 0.7, and at least 0.7. Those numbers are not the cost-derived recommendation above, and shouldn't be copied without calibration and evaluation. Verification might use an OTP or another appropriate method, with its own costs and completion rates.

Keep learning after deployment

I'd monitor confirmed fraud capture, losses, false alarms, score distributions, data quality, and latency, accounting for delayed labels. A drop in detection could reflect changing attackers, a data outage, or changing case mix. It should prompt investigation before an automatic retrain.

Daily or weekly retraining may be appropriate when enough reliable new outcomes exist. Investigator verdicts help, but the reviewed queue is a selected sample. I'd retain that context in training and evaluation. If attackers move from nighttime jewelry purchases to daytime electronics, I'd want the system to notice without blindly treating all electronics shoppers as suspicious.

In a design discussion, I'd explain the decision costs first, then the historical and velocity features, a rules and tree baseline, and the validation setup. I'd add sequences or graphs only for a demonstrated gap. The final test is whether the resulting policy reduces loss while treating legitimate customers reasonably, within the response time the product actually has.
